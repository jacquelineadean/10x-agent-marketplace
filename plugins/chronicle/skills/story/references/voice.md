# How the page reads

The page should read like a well-edited feature: specific, plain, confident where the
facts are, and open about what is unknown.

## Headlines and structure

- The page title is a name, two words at most: the project or subject ("Keystone").
- The dek states the point in two or three sentences with the key numbers as tokens.
- Each chapter title makes a claim or names a scene: "Three arches and a keystone",
  "The flood". Chart titles state what the chart shows as a finding: "Checkout latency
  fell the week checkout moved", not "Latency over time".
- Subtitles say what is plotted and in which unit.

## Sentences

- Short sentences, active voice, the subject first.
- Every number is a token. Round in the facts, not in the prose.
- Concrete nouns from the reader's world: "checkout", "the monolith", "Black Friday".
- No em or en dashes; use commas, colons, parentheses or a new sentence.
- No hype ("revolutionary", "world-class"), no hedging stacks, no rhetorical questions in
  body text.
- Name people only with their consent or in invented demo data; credit squads for work.
- Problems get the same plain treatment as wins: what happened, why, what changed.

## Captions and lessons

- Plate captions tie the painting to the facts: "Three arches carry traffic; the fourth,
  payments, waits for its keystone."
- A lesson is one or two sentences of practice the reader can reuse.

## The ask

End a status page with the decisions or help the authors need, each with its date and
its consequence, in one short paragraph or a short list.
